A Short Bio

Professor Lingqi Yan is an Associate Professor of Computer Science at Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence (MBZUAI). His research focuses on photorealistic rendering, including appearance modeling and representation, physical light transport theory, neural-aided rendering, and practical real-time ray tracing techniques. He received the 2019 ACM SIGGRAPH Outstanding Doctoral Dissertation Award and multiple best paper awards. His research has been widely adopted in industry and has contributed to multiple films that won the Academy Award for Best Visual Effects. He has also been serving on the editorial board of IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics (TVCG).

Research

+ + + =

My research is in Computer Graphics. I mainly aimed at rendering photo-realistic visual appearance (a.k.a. hard-core graphics) at real world complexity, building theoretical foundations mathematically and physically to reveal the principles of the visual world. I have brought original research topics to Computer Graphics, such as detailed rendering from microstructure, and real-time ray tracing with reconstruction. And I took the first steps exploiting neural approaches for physically based rendering.

As a Computer Graphics researcher, my dream is to present people an interactive computer-generated world to live in, just like the ones in the movies The Matrix and Ready Player One. To achieve that, I come up with my own Rendering Equation, as you can see above. I am prudently looking for outstanding graduate students to work on related research topics, including but not limited to physically-based / image-based rendering, real-time ray tracing, neural-aided rendering and realistic appearance modeling / acquisition. Let's work together, not to change the world, but to create one.

Recruitment and Collaboration


First of all, if you're looking at this page, it indicates that you're interested in working with me. I would like to thank you very much for this.

Starting Fall 2024, I plan to send out a couple of M.S. offers and zero to one Ph.D. offer each year. However, I will keep an eye on the submission pool, in case that there are truly exceptional applicants.

I sincerely hope that you apply only because you like Rendering, or in general, Computer Graphics, and you appreciate what I've been working on. I know you have a lot of questions. Let me organize them as Q-s (to me) and A-s (from me) below. Some of my answers can be REALLY HARSH, and I can foresee that some of you may turn away right after reading them. No problem, because I do want these words to act a pass of pre-screening. However, these are real thoughts and advices from me and I hope they can help you in your research career as well.

Q: What's your research interest?
A: First of all, DO NOT trust anyone's research interest. It can be anything unfounded. For example, I am interested in piloting a helicopter, and I'm not lying, so I can write it on my resume without feeling ashamed. But it doesn't in any sense indicate that I can actually fly a helicopter. When you look for someone's research, just look for what has been done and judge by yourself what she/he is really interested in and is capable of.
To me, my research is Rendering in Computer Graphics. And it always will be. However, I am not interested in Computer Vision and will not recruit any Computer Vision students, though I might be involved in my collaborators' research projects. While I honor the area of Computer Vision as much as you do, I must say that it is completely different from Computer Graphics. Please don't mix them. Also, I consider Machine Learning as a general tool. I would very much like to explore the possibility to apply it in Graphics, but wouldn't rely solely on it.

Q: What are the your requirements for me to work with you?
A: It is a must to have taken a Computer Graphics course. Strong knowledge in Math (esp. Calculus and Linear Algebra), Physics (esp. Optics), Programming (esp. C++ and OI/ICPC awards), Statistics. Participation in Computer Graphics research/engineering is strongly recommended.
I would like to further highlight the GRAPHICS-RELATED experience. For example, if you have written a renderer with correct bidirectional path tracing on your own, or if you have implemented several algorithms from SIGGRAPH papers in PBRT, or if you have (pre)published a paper in Pacific Graphics, you are awesome. As you can probably see already, I need proof that you are interested in Computer Graphics and you can do it well. That's it.
Experience in Python, diffusion models as well as general neural networks, and publications in Machine Learning and Computer Vision, will be neither useful nor harmful to me, and will simply be ignored. Just make sure there is still something left when you cross them off your resume.
I don't care much about letter grades and English test scores. But don't stand out in an opposite way.

Q: I don't have any Computer Graphics background, but I'm a quick learner / I work hard / I have a lot of CVPR papers, can I be your Ph.D. student?
A: No. And I question your logic. Do other students who have a good Computer Graphics background just sleep all day? Otherwise, how do you beat them and stand out?

Q: I haven't heard of you previously, but I have found that your research is interesting, can I be your Ph.D. student?
A: No. If it is the first time that you hear of me, I am pretty sure that you haven't done anything related to rendering research. So it is unlikely that I will recruit you as my Ph.D. student.

Q: I am an undergrad/master/Ph.D. student and I have a great idea, may I collaborate with you on that?
A: Yes, but not personally. Let your advisor (who must be a Computer Graphics researcher) contact me.

Q: Do you recruit interns in summer 2024?
A: No. I may have my own research plan. And I may need to take a complete break during the summer and spend more time with my family. I don't have time to guide you through your internship.
Also, it is difficult to finish a project on rendering in such short time, especially for junior students that haven't been properly trained. Therefore, it is impossible that you'll have a paper published with me from a summer internship.
Also, please do not reach out to anyone (my former advisors, collaborators, friends, etc.), whom I cannot refuse, to ask me to take you as a summer intern.

Q: Do you have any positions for visiting students/scholars in the 2024-2025 academic year?
A: I may consider. If you are a student, I need informal recommendations from your advisor. When you are sending me emails, please remember to cc your recommender. This is strictly enforced, or I will not reply to your email. Visiting scholars are welcome to contact me directly, but again, (core) Computer Graphics only.

Q: Will you recruit postdocs?
A: Fund permitting, yes.

Q: Can you give some examples of emails improperly contacting you?
A: "Of course I can imagine that you want to ask me if I am qualified in the first place. But everything has a start and no one was born experienced. If everyone recruits only experienced people, no one would."
"I’m in search of a PhD in computer science program for Fall 2021. Browsing your homepage https://www.cs.ucsb.edu/people/faculty/yan-0, I found your research area is fairly appealing to me and matches with my interest."

When Do I Consider Submitting Technical Reports


I always believe that technical reports (especially those non-anonymous) are harmful to the double blind reviewing process. The technical reports were not commonly seen in core rendering, but recently, even decent rendering researchers, both in the academia and in the industry, began to submit technical reports. Moreover, top conferences (SIGGRAPH, SIGGRAPH Asia, etc.) explicitly express that no penalties will be given based on existing Arxiv papers. Therefore, I really feel the pain deciding which side to stand on. As a result, I made the following rules for myself to determine whether to submit technical reports.

I would only consider submitting technical reports if at least one of the following conditions apply:

  1. I am not the corresponding author. Therefore, I don't decide.
  2. A coauthor of mine strongly suggests doing so, or has to do so based on e.g. company policies.
  3. I am submitting a non-rendering paper.
  4. I consider that my paper has been treated unfairly in a previous submission.

The last bullet is especially important. It happens quite often that a non-rendering expert would review and judge my submission with strong (and of course, factually wrong) opinion and confidence (out of nowhere). Once I determine that my submission was rejected because of this, I would consider immediately submitting a technical report.

Misc

I am a huge fan of video games. When I was in primary school, it was this specific game that convinced me to take rendering and Computer Graphics as my lifelong career. I regularly hit Legend in Hearthstone. At UC Berkeley, we had a Hearthstone team and we made to the playoffs in the TeSPA Hearthstone Collegiate National Championship. I also enjoy battle royal games. I play Apex Legends and reached Diamond rank consistently.

I play piano a little bit, but classic only (with a National Piano Certificate of Level 10 in China, topmost for non-professional amateurs). I'm especially fond of Chopin's. Here are some short recordings of my home performance (F. Chopin: Waltz Op. 64. No. 2 in C-Sharp Minor, Nocturne Op. 9 No. 1 in B-Flat Minor and Op. 9 No. 2 in E-Flat Major). I'll upload more as my website continues to update.

My legal name spelling should be Lingqi Yan, and I only use Ling-Qi Yan for publications (due to some lab traditions at Tsinghua University). I am now gradually standardizing on the spelling Lingqi Yan. My name is pronounced as Ling--Chi--Yen, and here are some funny mistakes about how people usually call me (and I like them all).
